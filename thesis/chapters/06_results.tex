\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:results}

This chapter first presents the results of the two image-analysis stages and then the evaluation of the domain-expert LLM, which is the central question of the thesis topic. It continues with an end-to-end case study of a real run of the lab assistant, and ends with a discussion of the research questions and the limitations.

\subsection{Stage 1: detection results}

On the 126-image TXL-PBC test split, the detector reached a mAP@0.50 of 0.985 and a mAP@0.50:0.95 of 0.876 (Table~\ref{tab:det}). White cells were found with a recall of 1.000, so every annotated white cell in the test split reaches the classifier. Red cells have the lowest recall (0.943), as expected for densely packed, overlapping cells, and platelets --- the smallest and rarest objects (49 test instances) --- have the lowest precision and mAP. The normalised confusion matrix is nearly diagonal; the errors are missed cells and false detections against the background rather than confusions between classes (Figure~\ref{fig:det}). The training and validation losses decreased steadily over the 50 epochs and the validation metrics levelled off without a late decline, so there is no sign of over-fitting (Figure~\ref{fig:yolo-curves}).

\subsection{Stage 2: classification and uncertainty results}

With the corrected protocol (v2), EfficientNet-B0 reached a top-1 accuracy of \textbf{98.60\,\%} on the 3{,}419 held-out test images (98.57\,\% with MC-Dropout averaging), with a macro-averaged F1 of 0.987 (Table~\ref{tab:cls}). The original run (v1), whose checkpoint had been selected on its own test images, reported 99.44\,\%; the difference of 0.84 percentage points is the size of the optimism that the flawed protocol introduced, and 98.60\,\% is the figure this thesis relies on. The best validation accuracy was 87.78\,\% after Phase~1 (frozen backbone, early stop at epoch 31) and 98.60\,\% after Phase~2, so unfreezing the backbone again added more than ten percentage points (Figure~\ref{fig:cls}).

The lowest recall belongs to the immature granulocyte (0.959), and the lowest precision to the neutrophil (0.968): most of the remaining errors are immature granulocytes classified as neutrophils (Figure~\ref{fig:cls}). This is morphologically plausible --- immature granulocytes are transitional forms on the way to mature neutrophils, and the boundary is also difficult for human reviewers --- and it is exactly the kind of case in which an uncertainty estimate is useful. Figure~\ref{fig:mc} illustrates the uncertainty levels on eight test images; it was produced with the original (v1) model, before the systematic evaluation of the v2 model that follows.

Three observations follow. First, the classifier is \emph{well calibrated} in-domain (ECE below 0.004), and MC-Dropout improves calibration only marginally over a single pass. Second, the uncertainty scores \emph{separate errors from correct predictions very well} (AUROC $\approx 0.97$): 95\,\% of the cells are LOW and 99.7\,\% of those are correct, whereas the 1.9\,\% of cells marked HIGH contain half of all errors. Third, the MEDIUM level is a weak spot of the current review rule: its accuracy is only 84.7\,\%, and its 15 errors reach the expert without a flag. Flagging MEDIUM as well would send 4.8\,\% of the cells for review and catch 82\,\% of the errors (40 of 49) --- a trade-off between workload and safety that should be set together with clinical users. All of these numbers describe images from the same analyser as the training data; under distribution shift both accuracy and calibration are expected to be worse (see the example below).

\paragraph{An example of domain shift.} The PBC images used for training come from a single analyser with standardised staining. Figure~\ref{fig:shift} shows the combined Stage~1 + Stage~2 output on an image from a different source, produced in the Stage-3 notebook. The white cell has a segmented nucleus and granular cytoplasm, the appearance of a mature granulocyte, yet the classifier labelled it \emph{erythroblast} with a probability of 0.90. This single example does not measure the size of the problem, but it illustrates why the benchmark accuracy should not be expected to transfer unchanged to smears from other laboratories.

\paragraph{In-domain questions.} Fine-tuning brought the model's answers much closer to the textbook reference answers. ROUGE-L rose from 0.170 to 0.455 ($+0.285$); the paired bootstrap 95\,\% confidence interval of the improvement is $[0.259,\ 0.313]$, and the fine-tuned model scored higher than the base model on \emph{every one} of the 100 questions. Semantic similarity rose from 0.775 to 0.832. On both measures the fine-tuned 8-billion-parameter model also exceeded GPT-4o (ROUGE-L difference $+0.253$, 95\,\% CI $[0.226,\ 0.281]$, higher on 99 of 100 questions). Its answers are about four times shorter than those of the other two systems (37 vs.\ 147--159 words) and close to the length of the reference answers (35 words).

These numbers must be read carefully. The reference answers were written by a GPT model from the textbook passages, in a particular concise style, and the fine-tuned model was trained on 1{,}898 answers in exactly that style. Part of the gain is therefore a gain in \emph{form}: length, wording and structure. ROUGE-L rewards this strongly; semantic similarity, which is less sensitive to wording, shows a smaller but still clear gain. GPT-4o's lower scores on these two measures do not mean that its answers are less correct --- it writes longer, more general explanations that overlap less with the short reference text. The LLM-judge ratings below confirm this.

\paragraph{External questions.} On the MedMCQA questions the fine-tuned model answered 61 of 100 correctly and the base model 56. The two models disagreed on 19 questions (base correct only: 7; fine-tuned correct only: 12), and the exact McNemar test gives $p = 0.36$: the difference is well within chance at this sample size. Fine-tuning on 1{,}898 textbook pairs therefore did not measurably change the model's general hematology knowledge --- in particular, it did not damage it. (Both models are well above the 25\,\% chance level; three of the base model's answers could not be parsed as a letter and count as wrong.)

\paragraph{LLM-judge ratings.} Table~\ref{tab:judge} shows the 300 ratings. Fine-tuning raised \emph{faithfulness} to the textbook answer by 0.43 points (95\,\% CI $[0.25,\ 0.63]$) but left \emph{clinical correctness} unchanged ($-0.07$, CI $[-0.25,\ 0.12]$). GPT-4o was rated best on both scales, and by a wide margin on correctness (4.67 vs.\ 3.90 for the fine-tuned model, difference $+0.77$, CI $[0.58,\ 0.96]$) --- the opposite of the ROUGE-L ranking. The judge's rationales for the fine-tuned model's lowest-rated answers name concrete medical errors, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:res-qual}.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} Together, the three kinds of evidence give a consistent picture: \emph{QLoRA fine-tuning taught the model how the domain expert should answer --- concisely, in the textbook's terminology and with a source line --- but it did not teach it new medical knowledge.} This agrees with the literature, which finds that retrieval is more effective than fine-tuning for injecting knowledge \cite{ovadia2024}, and it supports the design choice of building the runtime expert around retrieval and tools.

\paragraph{Comparison with the first, leaky run.} The first run (\path{results/domain_expert/}), in which most test questions had a sibling from the same passage in training, gave ROUGE-L 0.161 / 0.433 / 0.181 (base / ours / GPT-4o), a faithfulness gain of $+0.54$, a correctness gain of $+0.05$, and a GPT-4o lead over the fine-tuned model of $+0.29$ (faithfulness) and $+0.63$ (correctness). The leakage thus did not create the main finding, but it made the fine-tuned model look closer to GPT-4o than it is. The two runs use different test questions, so this comparison is indicative only.

\subsection{Qualitative analysis: citations and errors}\label{sec:res-qual}

\paragraph{Invented citations in the base model.} No source was given to the models, yet the base Llama model named a specific textbook, author or reference list in \textbf{68 of the 100} in-domain answers (flagged by the rule of Section~\ref{sec:eval} and checked by hand). Typical examples are:

\begin{quote}\small
``According to the textbook \emph{`Hematology: A Very Short Introduction'} by Patricia A. Tanner [1] \ldots'' \\
``References: [1] Lichtman, M.\,A., Kipps, T.\,J., \& Steinberg, D.\,A. (2014). Williams Hematology \ldots''
\end{quote}

The attributions are fabricated --- the model was never shown these books, and in several answers the same title is attributed to different authors --- but they look convincing. This is the failure mode described by Walters and Wilder for ChatGPT \cite{walters2023}, and it is caused here by the system prompt, which asks the model to ``cite'' a ``provided textbook reference'' that is not there. Neither the fine-tuned model nor GPT-4o named an invented book. GPT-4o, however, inserted a bare ``[Reference~$N$]'' marker in 37 answers (the base model in 13), pointing to a reference that did not exist --- a milder form of the same behaviour.

\paragraph{Uniform source lines in the fine-tuned model.} The fine-tuned model ended 99 of its 100 answers with a source line (Table~\ref{tab:de}), naming the first textbook in 92 answers and the second in 7. It usually chose the right book --- seven of the ten questions from the second book were labelled correctly --- which shows that it learned which topics belong to which book. But the line is learned format, not provenance: the model attaches it to correct and incorrect answers alike. Examples of incorrect answers that carry the source line are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Ham test}: the model describes it as a test for haemolytic disease of the newborn; it is the acidified-serum test that confirms paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria.
  \item \emph{Low MCV and MCH with normal MCHC}: the model suggests iron deficiency anaemia, whereas the reference answer points to thalassaemia (in iron deficiency the MCHC is also low).
  \item \emph{Vitamin B12 deficiency}: the model states that serum vitamin B12 is \emph{normal} in megaloblastic anaemia due to B12 deficiency; it is reduced.
  \item \emph{Platelet count of $90 \times 10^9$/L} (qualitative question): the model correctly says that this is not always true thrombocytopenia, but claims that platelet clumping makes the count spuriously \emph{raised}; clumping makes it falsely low.
\end{itemize}

The fine-tuned model therefore looks more authoritative than the base model while being no more knowledgeable. For a domain expert this is an important and somewhat uncomfortable finding: \textbf{a citation produced by a language model is not evidence}. A citation is only useful if it points to a passage that was actually retrieved and can be opened, as in the RAG agent, where every \code{[Reference N]} corresponds to a logged tool result.

Both models recognise the typical patterns (neutrophilic leukocytosis with toxic granulation suggests infection or inflammation; a microcytic anaemia with target cells suggests a hemoglobinopathy). The fine-tuned model is shorter and more decisive; the base model is longer, lists more possibilities, and often decorates its answer with invented reference numbers.

\subsection{End-to-end case study: an over-interpreted differential}\label{sec:case}

This section analyses one complete run of the system in its default configuration (GPT-4o agent) on an uploaded $640 \times 480$ smear image, made on 28 April 2026 with the original (v1) classifier and before the minimum-cell rule existed. It is analysed in detail because it shows both the strengths of the design and a failure that the safety logic did not catch. Its raw output files were later overwritten by a re-run on the same image (see below); the values were recorded from the original output (abridged in Appendix~C), and the run's log (\path{logs/pipeline_20260428_140721.log}) confirms the counts, the 50/50 differential, that no cell was flagged and that review was \emph{not} required.

\paragraph{What the system did.} Stage~1 detected 17 red cells and \textbf{2 white cells}. Stage~2 classified one white cell as an immature granulocyte (confidence 0.955, entropy 0.216) and the other as a monocyte (confidence 0.972, entropy 0.146); both received the uncertainty level LOW, so no cell was flagged. The agent then made five tool calls: it looked up the reference ranges of immature granulocytes (0--0.5\,\%) and monocytes (2--8\,\%), read the uncertainty summary, and searched the knowledge base for ``causes of increased immature granulocytes'' and ``causes of monocytosis''. Its final report stated:

\begin{quote}\small
``The peripheral blood smear shows a significant increase in immature granulocytes (50\,\%) and monocytes (50\,\%), which is highly abnormal. [\ldots] Immature granulocytes (IG) at 50\,\%, significantly above the normal range of 0--0.5\,\% [Reference 6].'' --- Recommendation: ``Consider bone marrow biopsy if clinically indicated.'' --- \code{confidence\_assessment}: HIGH, \code{requires\_expert\_review}: \textbf{false}.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{What went wrong.} Three problems can be identified.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The sample was far too small.} A differential expresses each cell type as a percentage of the white cells counted. Routine manual differentials count at least 100 cells, and the reference method of CLSI H20-A2 counts several hundred \cite{clsi2007h20}. Two cells cannot establish any percentage; ``50\,\% immature granulocytes'' is an artefact of dividing by two. The system prompt told the agent that the counts are per microscope field, but it did not tell it to check the number of cells, and nothing in the code did.
  \item \textbf{A citation was misattributed.} The retrieved chunk labelled Reference~6 is about hypersegmented neutrophils in megaloblastic anaemia. The range 0--0.5\,\% actually came from the reference-range tool. The claim was correct, but its citation pointed to the wrong evidence --- the kind of error that only becomes visible because the tool trace is recorded.
  \item \textbf{The review flag stayed false.} Both cells had low MC-Dropout uncertainty, so the deterministic rule did not fire, and the model itself set the flag to false while recommending a bone-marrow biopsy. The uncertainty of the \emph{individual cells} was low; what was uncertain was the \emph{differential as a whole}, and the rule did not measure that.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Lessons.} The case shows that the design choices of the thesis --- a recorded tool trace, retrieved passages that can be checked, uncertainty passed forward --- make such failures \emph{detectable}: every step above could be reconstructed from the saved output. But it also shows that per-cell uncertainty is not enough. The fix is simple and deterministic, and it has since been implemented (Section~\ref{sec:safety}): whenever fewer than a minimum number of white cells (by default 100, which in practice requires several fields of view) have been classified, the pipeline forces \code{requires\_expert\_review} to true and adds an \code{INSUFFICIENT\_CELL\_COUNT} flag, and the reasoners are told not to interpret the percentages. Applied to this case (2 cells), the rule sets the flag to true; this is covered by an automated test. When asked about a two-cell differential directly (Table~\ref{tab:qual}), the base model at least called the classification incomplete; in the agent, the structured prompt and the tool outputs led the model to compute and interpret percentages. This is a strong argument for putting such domain rules into code rather than relying on the model's judgement.

\paragraph{Re-run with the corrected system.} On 29 September 2026 the same image was analysed again with the corrected (v2) classifier and the minimum-cell rule in place (output in \path{results/stage1_detection.json}, \path{stage2_classification.json} and \path{stage3_reasoning.json}). Detection was unchanged (17 red, 2 white cells). The v2 classifier was far less certain about the first cell (immature granulocyte, confidence 0.42, entropy 1.23, HIGH) while the monocyte stayed confident (0.98, LOW). The pipeline forced \code{requires\_expert\_review} to true with \code{HIGH\_UNCERTAINTY} and \code{INSUFFICIENT\_CELL\_COUNT} flags, and the agent rated its confidence LOW; its text still quoted the two 50\,\% values, though now as based on too few cells. The rule did its job, and the wording shows again why the decision must not be left to the model.

\subsection{Discussion of the research questions}

\paragraph{RQ1 --- blood image classification.} Yes, on the benchmark data. Detection reached a mAP@0.50 of 0.985 with perfect white-cell recall, and classification 98.60\,\% accuracy under a clean train/validation/test protocol (the earlier 99.44\,\% was 0.84 points too optimistic). Every classified cell carries an uncertainty level and a Grad-CAM map, and the uncertainty is informative: it separates errors from correct predictions with an AUROC of 0.97, and HIGH-uncertainty cells contain half of all errors although they make up only 1.9\,\% of the cells. How well this transfers to smears from other laboratories was not measured; the single out-of-distribution example in Figure~\ref{fig:shift} suggests caution.

\paragraph{RQ2 --- building the domain expert with RAG.} A general LLM was turned into a hematology expert by combining a curated textbook knowledge base, domain tools and a structured output format. The resulting agent produces interpretations with retrievable citations and a complete, auditable tool trace (Figures~\ref{fig:ui-a}--\ref{fig:ui-b}). Retrieval itself was measured: with a 512-token embedding model, the source passage of a question is among the five retrieved chunks for 77\,\% of the questions (67\,\% with the original model). The case study shows that this design makes errors visible but does not prevent them; domain rules such as a minimum cell count must be enforced in code.

\paragraph{RQ3 --- domain fine-tuning.} QLoRA fine-tuning of Llama-3.1-8B for under an hour on a free GPU produced a model whose answers match textbook reference answers much better than those of the base model and even of GPT-4o (ROUGE-L 0.455 vs.\ 0.170 and 0.202), and which no longer invents book references. The LLM judge agreed: faithfulness to the textbook improved ($+0.43$), clinical correctness did not ($-0.07$, not significant), and GPT-4o remained clearly the most correct system. Accuracy on external exam questions did not change significantly (61\,\% vs.\ 56\,\%, $p = 0.36$), and the model learned to attach a source line regardless of correctness. Fine-tuning is therefore suitable for adapting the \emph{behaviour} of an open, self-hostable expert, but it must be combined with retrieval to supply knowledge and real provenance.

\paragraph{RQ4 --- safety and auditability.} The system passes all safety-relevant information forward and forces expert review when any cell is uncertain. The case study showed the limits of the original rule: it reacted to uncertain \emph{cells}, not to an unreliable \emph{differential}. A minimum-cell rule was therefore added. Auditability worked as intended --- the misattributed citation and the faulty reasoning could be traced step by step.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synthetic reference answers.} The in-domain test answers were generated by a GPT model from the textbook, not written by hematologists. They may contain errors, and they favour a model trained on the same style. Some questions refer to ``the passage'', and a few ask about the second book's list of contributors (for example, who heads a university division) rather than about hematology --- artefacts of the generation.
  \item \textbf{Data leakage in the first fine-tuning run} was corrected (Section~\ref{sec:qa-data}), but 20 of the 100 test chunks still share a 40-word overlap with a neighbouring training chunk.
  \item \textbf{Small, noisy external test.} The MedMCQA subset has 100 questions, about a quarter of which are only loosely related to hematology; small differences cannot be detected with this sample size.
  \item \textbf{No human ratings.} Correctness was assessed with automatic similarity measures, an LLM judge (GPT-4o-mini) and by hand for a few answers; no clinician evaluated the answers. An LLM judge has its own biases, and one from the same vendor as GPT-4o may favour GPT-4o's style.
  \item \textbf{Training format of the fine-tuned model.} The training examples contain no retrieved passage, so the model was not trained to read and cite evidence. The fine-tuned model was evaluated stand-alone, not inside the agent.
  \item \textbf{Classifier evaluation.} The corrected (v2) split is stratified by class but at image level, not by donor, so cells from the same person can appear in training and test data. The original (v1) evaluation, which selected its checkpoint on the test images, is reported only for comparison. (A mismatch in the inference code, which re-created the second dropout layer with $p = 0.2$ instead of the $p = 0.3$ used in training, was found during this work and corrected; the case-study run predates the correction.)
  \item \textbf{No external validation of the vision stages.} Both vision datasets were recorded under controlled conditions; smears from other microscopes, stains or laboratories may reduce performance substantially.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty thresholds and review rule} were set by hand. They were evaluated on in-distribution test data (Table~\ref{tab:unc}), where MEDIUM cells turned out to be noticeably less reliable than LOW cells but are not flagged; their behaviour under distribution shift is unknown.
  \item \textbf{Retrieval evaluation on synthetic questions.} The questions were generated from the chunks themselves, so hit rates are optimistic for real queries, and the embedding model was chosen on the same questions, without a separate test set.
  \item \textbf{Copyright of the knowledge base.} The textbooks are copyrighted and cannot be redistributed, which limits exact reproduction of the knowledge base by others.
\end{itemize}
