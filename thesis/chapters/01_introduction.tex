\section{Introduction}

This thesis was written on the topic \emph{Development of a domain expert using a large language model like ChatGPT}. It addresses the topic by building and evaluating a concrete system, a \emph{hybrid multimodal lab assistant} for the analysis of peripheral blood smears, in which deep-learning models classify the blood cells in a microscope image and a large language model, grounded in hematology textbooks through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), acts as the domain expert that interprets the findings.

Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT have changed how people look for information. Models of the GPT-4 class answer many medical questions at the level of a well-read student and reach high scores on medical licensing-style benchmarks \cite{singhal2023}. It is therefore tempting to use such a model directly as an ``expert'' in a specialised field. In a clinical field, however, a general-purpose model has three weaknesses that make it unsuitable as an expert on its own. First, it can produce \emph{unsupported claims}: fluent statements that are wrong or cannot be verified, a behaviour usually called hallucination \cite{ji2023}. Second, it offers \emph{no provenance}: a user cannot see where an answer came from, and when asked for sources a general model may even invent references that do not exist \cite{walters2023}. Third, it has \emph{no access to the case}: a chat model does not see the patient's data --- here, the cells on a smear image --- so its answer is generic rather than case-specific.

In the sense used in this thesis, a \emph{domain expert} is an LLM-based system that (i) answers within one field, (ii) grounds its statements in a curated body of domain literature and cites it, (iii) can inspect case-specific evidence through tools, and (iv) signals when a human expert must take over. The lab assistant supplies the case-specific evidence by analysing the image; the domain expert supplies the knowledge needed to interpret it.

The word \emph{multimodal} is used here at the level of the system: the image and the text are processed by different specialised models and fused in the reasoning stage, rather than by a single vision-language model.

\subsection{Application domain: the peripheral blood smear}

The peripheral blood smear is one of the oldest and still one of the most informative laboratory tests in hematology. A drop of blood is spread on a glass slide, stained with a Romanowsky-type stain and examined under a microscope. From a single smear a trained reviewer estimates red-cell morphology and platelet numbers and counts the proportions of the five main types of white blood cells (WBC): neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils. The reviewer may also find immature granulocytes, blasts or atypical lymphocytes, which can be the first sign of infection, leukaemia or bone-marrow disease.

Manual smear review is slow, depends on the experience of the observer and is not equally available everywhere. This has motivated a large body of work on automated cell detection and classification with deep learning \cite{acevedo2019,habibzadeh2018}. Most such systems, however, stop at a label for each cell. Turning a set of labels into a \emph{clinical interpretation} --- what the differential means, what could explain it, and whether it is reliable enough to act on --- requires domain knowledge. Hematology is therefore a natural field for a hybrid assistant: it combines visual, case-specific evidence, which image models can extract, with textbook knowledge, which a domain-expert LLM can supply.

\subsection{Problem statement}

The thesis addresses four gaps.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cell classifiers stop at labels.} Automated smear analysis produces a label per cell, but not an interpretation of the differential as a whole.
  \item \textbf{A general LLM is not a domain expert.} Prompting a model such as GPT-4o gives fluent answers, but without grounding, citations or access to case data.
  \item \textbf{Dependence on a commercial API.} A domain expert that only works through a paid, hosted model cannot be used where data must stay on site. An open, self-hostable alternative is needed, and it has to be evaluated rather than assumed to work.
  \item \textbf{Automated image findings are silent about their reliability.} If the expert reasons over cell labels without knowing how certain they are, it can confidently interpret classification errors. Uncertainty must be measured and passed on.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research questions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline]
  \item[RQ1] Can standard deep-learning models (YOLOv8, EfficientNet-B0) detect and classify blood cells in a smear image accurately, and attach an uncertainty estimate and a saliency map to every classified white cell?
  \item[RQ2] How can a general-purpose LLM be turned into a hematology domain expert using retrieval-augmented generation and domain-specific tools, and what does the resulting assistant produce on real smear images?
  \item[RQ3] Does QLoRA fine-tuning of an open-weight model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) on textbook-grounded question--answer pairs measurably improve its hematology answers compared with the same model without fine-tuning, on held-out and on external questions?
  \item[RQ4] Can the combined system make its reasoning inspectable (citations, tool traces) and hand difficult cases to a human through a deterministic review rule --- and where does this fail?
\end{description}

\subsection{Contributions and main findings}

The contribution of the thesis is a documented, reproducible hybrid system and an evaluation of its domain-expert component, not a new neural-network architecture. Concretely:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{blood image classification pipeline}: a YOLOv8s cell detector (mAP@0.50 = 0.985 on TXL-PBC) and an EfficientNet-B0 white-cell classifier (99.44\,\% on the PBC hold-out, an optimistic figure; Section~\ref{sec:method}) whose predictions carry Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty and Grad-CAM saliency (Sections~\ref{sec:stage1}, \ref{sec:stage2}).
  \item A \textbf{retrieval-grounded knowledge base} of 1{,}049 passages from two hematology textbooks, embedded into a vector store so that statements can be cited to a source (Section~\ref{sec:kb}).
  \item A \textbf{tool-using domain-expert agent} built on GPT-4o with five hematology tools, which returns a structured interpretation together with a complete tool trace (Section~\ref{sec:agent}).
  \item A \textbf{fine-tuned open-weight domain expert}: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct adapted with QLoRA on 1{,}890 textbook-grounded question--answer pairs and evaluated on 208 held-out pairs and on 100 external MedMCQA questions (Sections~\ref{sec:ft}, \ref{sec:res-de}).
  \item A \textbf{deterministic expert-review rule} and an \textbf{honest failure analysis} of a real case in which the assistant over-interpreted a two-cell differential, which led to an additional minimum-cell rule (Sections~\ref{sec:safety}, \ref{sec:case}).
  \item A \textbf{reproducible software artefact}: a configuration-driven Python library, a REST backend, a web interface, Colab notebooks and 34 automated tests.
\end{enumerate}

The main empirical findings about the domain expert are the following. Fine-tuning made the open model's answers much closer to the textbook reference answers (semantic similarity 0.752 $\rightarrow$ 0.831, ROUGE-L 0.161 $\rightarrow$ 0.433 on 100 held-out questions, with a higher ROUGE-L on every one of them), and on this measure it also exceeded GPT-4o (0.774 and 0.181). It did not, however, improve accuracy on external multiple-choice questions (56\,\% vs.\ 57\,\%, a non-significant difference), so fine-tuning taught the textbook's answering style rather than new medical knowledge. Without fine-tuning, the open model named a specific textbook, author or edition in 52 of 100 answers although no source had been provided --- invented citations. The fine-tuned model stopped doing this, but it attached a source line to every answer, including incorrect ones. Citations produced by a model are therefore not evidence by themselves, which is why the assistant's runtime reasoning is built around retrieval of real passages (RAG) and a tool trace rather than around fine-tuning alone.
